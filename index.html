Heat · Water Cycle · States of Matter

Q1.

Siti set up the following experiment. She placed two identical wet towels — Towel P and Towel Q — on a clothesline. Towel P was hung in direct sunlight outdoors. Towel Q was hung in a sealed, air-conditioned room at 18°C. Both towels had the same mass of water absorbed at the start. The towels were weighed every 30 minutes.

Line graph: Mass of water remaining in each towel over time

Solid red line = Towel P (outdoor, sunlight); Dashed blue line = Towel Q (indoor, air-conditioned)

Based on the line graph, which statement best explains the difference in drying rates between Towel P and Towel Q?

  1. Towel P dried faster because sunlight increases the surface area of the towel, allowing more water to evaporate.
  2. Towel P dried faster because the higher outdoor temperature increases the rate of evaporation, and moving air carries water vapour away faster.
  3. Towel Q dried slower because the air-conditioned room has higher humidity, which increases the rate of condensation on the towel.
  4. Both towels dried at the same rate because the mass of water absorbed was identical at the start; the difference shown is due to measurement error.

Which sequence correctly describes the main energy conversions from start to finish?

Experimental Design · Fair Test · Plant Transport System

OEQ 1 — Fair Test & Experimental Analysis [5 marks]

Scenario: Mei Ling wanted to investigate whether the amount of light affects the height that coloured water travels up a plant stem. She used four identical stalks of celery, each cut to 10 cm. She placed them in four identical beakers, each containing 50 mL of red-coloured water (same concentration of food dye).

She set up the four samples under different conditions:

  • Setup A: Placed in a dark cupboard (no light)
  • Setup B: Placed under a 25-watt lamp, 20 cm away
  • Setup C: Placed under a 60-watt lamp, 20 cm away
  • Setup D: Placed outdoors in direct sunlight

After 3 hours, Mei Ling measured the height the red colour had travelled up each celery stalk. The hypothetical results are shown below.

Setup Light condition Height red colour travelled up stalk (cm)
A Dark cupboard (no light) 0.5
B 25-watt lamp 2.0
C 60-watt lamp 3.5
D Direct sunlight 5.0

(a) Identify the changed variable (independent variable) and list three variables that must be kept the same to ensure this is a fair test. [2 marks]

(b) Mei Ling's teacher said she needed a control setup. Explain the purpose of a control setup in this experiment and identify which setup serves as the control. [1 mark]

(c) Based on the results in the table, state the conclusion Mei Ling should draw. Explain why more light causes the coloured water to travel higher up the stem, using the correct scientific concept. [2 marks]

Real-World Application · Heat Transfer · Adaptations

OEQ 2 — Real-World Application [5 marks]

Scenario: The Saharan silver ant (Cataglyphis bombycina) is one of the most heat-tolerant insects on Earth. It forages for food in the Sahara Desert during the hottest part of the day — when sand surface temperatures exceed 70°C — for only 2 to 3 minutes at a time before retreating underground. Its body is covered with tiny, triangular silver-coloured hairs that are shaped like tiny prisms.

Scientists discovered two remarkable facts about these hairs:

  1. The silver hairs reflect intense sunlight away from the ant's body, especially in the near-infrared range.
  2. The hairs are angled so that hot air rises away from the ant's body surface through a process similar to a chimney effect, allowing heat to escape.

In contrast, ants of the same species that have had their silver hairs removed experimentally were observed to survive for far less time on the hot sand.

(a) Explain how the silver colour of the hairs helps the ant survive on the hot desert sand. Use the correct scientific concept relating to heat and light. [2 marks]

(b) The ant also has very long legs compared to its body size. Explain how having long legs is an adaptive advantage in this extreme heat environment. [1 mark]

(c) The scientist compared the silver ant to a "thermal flask" that works in reverse — instead of keeping heat in, it pushes heat out. Explain how the chimney-effect hair structure and the short foraging time (2–3 minutes) work together to prevent the ant's body from overheating. Use the concept of heat gain and heat loss. [2 marks]

Section A: Multiple-Choice Answers

Q1 — Answer: (B) 2 marks

Full-credit rationale: Towel P dried faster because the higher outdoor temperature increases the rate of evaporation, and moving air (wind) carries water vapour away from the towel surface, maintaining a steep concentration gradient.

Common Trap

Option (A) is the most popular wrong answer. Students confuse "sunlight increases temperature" with "sunlight changes surface area". Sunlight does not alter the physical size of the towel — it raises the thermal energy of the water, increasing evaporation. Option (C) attracts students who conflate condensation with slow evaporation — the air-conditioned room is actually less humid (cooler air holds less moisture), but the still air and lower temperature reduce the evaporation rate. Option (D) ignores the clear visual evidence of diverging lines on the graph.

Q2 — Answer: (C) 2 marks

Full-credit rationale: Water at height has gravitational potential energy. As it flows down, this converts to kinetic energy (moving water turns the turbine). The turbine drives the generator, producing electrical energy. At the factory, the electricity powers machinery, which converts to heat energy (friction) and kinetic energy (moving parts). Not all energy is converted usefully — some is lost as heat due to friction.

Common Trap

Option (A) is the top distractor. Students often correctly identify the first three conversions but forget that the factory uses the electrical energy to do work — it doesn't convert it back into kinetic energy as the primary output. The question asks for the main energy conversions end-to-end: the useful work at the factory produces heat (friction, thermal processes) and kinetic energy (machinery). Option (D) skips kinetic energy entirely, confusing potential energy directly converting to electrical energy, which is physically impossible without an intermediate mechanical stage.

Q3 — Answer: (B) 2 marks

Full-credit rationale: From the data table: Beaker W (80°C) lost 24 mL, Beaker X (60°C) lost 16 mL, Beaker Y (40°C) lost 8 mL, and Beaker Z (20°C) lost 4 mL. This shows a clear pattern — the higher the starting temperature, the more water evaporated. This is because heat energy increases the rate of evaporation — the water vapour gains enough energy to escape into the surrounding air.

Common Trap

Option (D) is the classic "right answer, wrong reason" distractor. Students who select (D) correctly read the data pattern (higher temp → more evaporation) but confuse evaporation with condensation. Condensation is the opposite process — gas turning to liquid — and does not occur in the beaker. Option (A) reverses the relationship entirely. Option (C) is chosen by students who don't trust the data and invent confounding variables (beaker size) that the question explicitly controls for ("identical glass beakers").

Section B: Open-Ended Marking Schemes

OEQ 1 — Fair Test & Experimental Analysis 5 marks total

(a) Changed variable + 3 controlled variables [2 marks]

[1] Changed (independent) variable: The amount/intensity of light each celery stalk is exposed to.
[1] Any three of the following controlled (constant) variables:
  • Type/species and size of celery stalk (same length, 10 cm)
  • Volume of coloured water (50 mL in each beaker)
  • Concentration of food dye
  • Type/size of beaker
  • Duration of the experiment (3 hours)
  • Ambient temperature / air movement (all set up in the same location category — though D is outdoors, this is a limitation; accept "surrounding temperature" if addressed)
Common Trap

Students frequently list "the amount of water" as the changed variable — this is wrong; the water volume is controlled. The most common omission is failing to identify duration as a controlled variable. Some students also confuse "changed variable" (independent) with "measured variable" (dependent: height of red colour).

(b) Purpose of control setup [1 mark]

[1] A control setup is used for comparison to show that the result (red colour travelling up the stem) is caused by the changed variable (light) and not by some other factor. Setup A (the dark cupboard) serves as the control because it removes the light variable entirely — it shows what happens under baseline conditions.
Common Trap

Many students say the control is "Setup B" because it has the weakest light. A control must eliminate the changed variable, not merely reduce it. Setup A (no light at all) is the true control. Students also frequently state the purpose of a control as "to make it a fair test" — this is too vague. The control is for comparison/baseline, not for ensuring fairness (that is the role of controlled variables).

(c) Conclusion + scientific explanation [2 marks]

[1] Conclusion: The greater the amount of light the celery stalk receives, the higher the red-coloured water travels up the stem.
[1] Explanation: More light causes the plant to photosynthesise faster, which increases the rate of transpiration (water loss through stomata). As water evaporates from the leaves, more coloured water is drawn up the stem through the xylem vessels to replace the water lost.
Common Trap

The most common error is stating the conclusion as "more light makes the celery grow taller" — confusing the transport of coloured water with plant growth. Students also explain the result using "capillary action" alone without mentioning transpiration pull — while capillary action plays a role, the PSLE marking scheme expects students to link light → photosynthesis → stomata open → transpiration increases → water drawn up xylem. Omitting transpiration loses the explanation mark.

OEQ 2 — Real-World Application: Saharan Silver Ant 5 marks total

(a) Silver hairs & heat survival [2 marks]

[1] Evidence from question: The silver hairs reflect intense sunlight away from the ant's body, especially in the near-infrared range (stated in the passage).
[1] Concept application: Light-coloured / shiny surfaces reflect more heat than dark surfaces. By reflecting sunlight away, the silver hairs reduce the amount of heat energy absorbed by the ant's body, keeping its body temperature lower.
Common Trap

Students often write "the silver hairs make the ant cooler" without specifying the mechanism. The marking scheme requires the concept that light/shiny surfaces are better reflectors of heat and light — a core P6 Heat topic. Another frequent error is attributing the cooling to the colour alone without mentioning reflection of radiant heat. Some students also confuse this with insulation (trapping air), which is the opposite — the silver hairs reflect heat away, not trap it.

(b) Long legs as adaptive advantage [1 mark]

[1] Long legs keep the ant's body further away from the hot sand surface, reducing the amount of heat conducted from the sand to the ant's body. This keeps the ant's body temperature lower, allowing it to survive longer on the 70°C sand.
Common Trap

Students frequently answer "long legs help the ant run faster and escape" — this addresses behavioural advantage but misses the thermal advantage the question targets. The key concept is heat conduction from the sand surface: greater distance = less conductive heat transfer. Students who only mention "running fast" without linking to heat gain from the sand lose the mark.

(c) Chimney-effect + short foraging time = heat management [2 marks]

[1] The chimney-effect hair structure allows hot air to rise and escape from the ant's body surface, increasing the rate of heat loss from the body. This works like a ventilation system: hot air (less dense) rises through the angled hairs and escapes, while cooler air replaces it.
[1] The short foraging time (2–3 minutes) ensures the ant returns underground before heat gain from the environment exceeds its heat loss. If it stayed out longer, the continuous radiant and conductive heat gain would overwhelm the chimney-effect cooling, and the ant's body temperature would rise to lethal levels.
Common Trap

The most sophisticated trap here: students describe the chimney effect correctly but fail to explain why the foraging time matters — they treat it as a separate, unconnected fact. The mark requires students to show the dynamic balance between heat gain and heat loss: the chimney effect increases heat loss rate, but in a 70°C environment, heat gain rate will eventually exceed heat loss rate. The 2–3 minute limit is the time window before that threshold is crossed. Students who don't use the terms "heat gain" and "heat loss" lose at least one mark even if the explanation is conceptually correct.
